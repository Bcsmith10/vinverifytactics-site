Plain-English guide

California VIN verification,
explained.

What it is, who can do it, when you need one, and what to have ready. Written by a licensed California vehicle verifier, in plain language, and checked against the DMV's own form and manual.

The basics

What is a VIN verification?

A VIN verification (the DMV calls it a "verification of vehicle") is a hands-on inspection. An authorized person walks up to the vehicle, finds the VIN on it, checks that the number and the vehicle's basic details match its ownership paperwork, and records what they see on Form REG 31.

The DMV can ask for one before it finishes processing a registration application. It's about confirming that the vehicle in front of the DMV is the vehicle on paper. The form is not filled out by the vehicle's owner. It says so right at the top.

Who is allowed to do it?

According to the form itself, the verification must be done by an authorized DMV representative, a California licensed vehicle verifier, an authorized auto club employee, or a peace officer who has been trained to do it. The verifier must physically inspect the vehicle, and their signature must be a real, handwritten signature. A typed or stamped signature isn't accepted.

When might you need one?

The DMV, or your registration service, will tell you when a verification is required. Common situations include bringing a vehicle in from another state and registering a vehicle that has no California record. The DMV's manual also says one is required when a vehicle does not meet California emission standards. If you aren't sure, ask the DMV or your registration service before booking anyone.

The inspection

What gets checked?

The REG 31 has nine numbered sections, and every one has to be completed. In everyday terms, the verifier looks at:

  • The VIN itself: where it is on the vehicle, how the plate is attached, what type it is, and whether it looks original and undamaged.
  • The federal safety label (usually on the door jamb): whether its VIN agrees with the vehicle's VIN and whether the label looks genuine and intact.
  • The odometer: the mileage exactly as displayed, and whether it's in miles or kilometers.
  • The ownership document: the VIN on the title, registration card, MSO/MCO or similar paperwork is compared with the vehicle.
  • The emissions label: what the label under the hood says about California or federal standards, for the vehicles that require one.
  • Basic vehicle details: body type, fuel, weight where it matters, and plates if there are any.

A quick lesson in VINs

  • Vehicles from the 1981 model year on have a 17-character VIN. The same goes for trailers from 1985 on.
  • The characters aren't random. The 9th is a "check digit" worked out from the others by a formula. If a character is misread or altered, the check usually fails, which is one way a bad VIN gets caught.
  • The 10th character shows the model year, for vehicles from 1980 on.
  • The DMV's form says the federal safety label should not be used as the primary source of the VIN. The VIN on the vehicle itself comes first.
Private verifiers

What a licensed private verifier can and can't do

A private verifier like us is allowed to verify nonresident vehicles, and most other vehicles and trailers if they have supporting ownership documents (not used motorcycles). Those documents are things like a title, an MCO/MSO, or a registration card.

A private verifier cannot verify:

  • a vehicle whose federal safety label is missing, damaged, illegible, altered, doesn't agree with the VIN, or doesn't match the documents;
  • revived salvage or revived junk vehicles;
  • specially constructed (custom-built, kit or replica) vehicles;
  • vehicles with ownership documents from a foreign country;
  • motorcycles with an engine change;
  • a used motorcycle last registered in California whose record no longer exists;
  • any 1981-or-newer vehicle with a VIN shorter than 17 characters, which goes to the CHP.

In those cases the vehicle needs to be inspected by the CHP or the DMV instead. Our booking form screens for these before you pay anything.

Getting ready

What to have ready

  • The vehicle, parked where the verifier can walk around it and open the doors.
  • The ownership document for it: title, registration card, or MSO/MCO.
  • Keys, so the odometer can be read.
  • The VIN plate and the door-jamb label uncovered and visible.

After the verification

The verifier hands you the signed REG 31. You take that original to the DMV (or give it to your registration service) with the rest of your paperwork. We can't submit it for you.

Can I verify my own vehicle?

No. The form is not to be completed by the applicant, and the verification must be done by an authorized person.
